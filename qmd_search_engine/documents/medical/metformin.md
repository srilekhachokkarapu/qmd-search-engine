# Metformin

## 1. Overview

Metformin is an oral antihyperglycemic medicine in the biguanide class.

It is widely used to help manage type 2 diabetes mellitus, together with individualized nutrition, physical activity and other diabetes-care measures.

It helps lower blood glucose and does not usually cause hypoglycaemia when used alone. Low blood glucose can occur when it is combined with certain other diabetes medicines.

This document is educational and is not a treatment plan.

## 2. Drug Classification

- Therapeutic class: Antidiabetic / antihyperglycemic
- Pharmacological class: Biguanide
- Generic name: Metformin
- Main indication: Management of type 2 diabetes, according to local approvals and clinical guidance

## 3. Common Uses

Metformin is used to:

- Improve blood glucose control in people with type 2 diabetes.
- Support diabetes management alongside nutrition and physical activity.
- Form part of a combination treatment plan when one medicine is not sufficient.

In some countries or clinical circumstances, clinicians may prescribe metformin for other indications.

Metformin is not a treatment for type 1 diabetes or diabetic ketoacidosis.

## 4. Mechanism of Action

Metformin lowers blood glucose through several actions:

- Reduces glucose production by the liver.
- Improves the body's response to insulin and insulin sensitivity.
- Reduces some intestinal glucose absorption.

It does not work by forcing the pancreas to release insulin.

## 5. Available Dosage Forms

Products may include:

- Immediate-release tablets
- Extended-release or modified-release tablets
- Oral solution in some markets
- Combination tablets containing metformin with another diabetes medicine

Different formulations may have different administration instructions.

Extended-release tablets often need to be swallowed whole. Follow the exact product leaflet.

## 6. Use and Administration Principles

- Take metformin exactly as prescribed.
- Many oral formulations are taken with meals to reduce gastrointestinal side effects.
- Take it at consistent times as directed.
- Do not crush, chew or split an extended-release tablet unless its product information explicitly permits this.
- Do not change the dose based on a single blood glucose reading without an agreed care plan.
- Keep scheduled appointments and laboratory monitoring.
- Ask the prescriber for a plan for significant vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, dehydration or inability to eat and drink normally.

Temporary interruption may be appropriate in some acute situations, but the clinician should give individualized instructions.

## 7. Common Side Effects

Common gastrointestinal effects, especially when treatment begins or the dose changes, may include:

- Diarrhoea
- Nausea or vomiting
- Abdominal discomfort
- Gas or bloating
- Indigestion
- Reduced appetite
- Metallic or altered taste

Some people develop low vitamin B12 levels during long-term treatment.

## 8. Serious Risks and Warnings

### Lactic Acidosis

Metformin-associated lactic acidosis is rare but potentially life-threatening.

Risk is higher in certain circumstances, including:

- Significant kidney impairment
- Severe dehydration
- Serious infection
- Low oxygen states
- Some liver or heart conditions
- Heavy alcohol use
- Certain procedures and medicines

Possible warning symptoms include:

- Unusual extreme tiredness or weakness
- Muscle pain
- Trouble breathing or deep, rapid breathing
- Severe nausea, vomiting or abdominal pain
- Feeling cold, dizzy or faint
- Unusually slow or fast heartbeat

These symptoms need urgent medical assessment.

### Hypoglycaemia

Metformin alone does not commonly cause hypoglycaemia.

Low blood glucose may occur when it is combined with insulin or certain other diabetes medicines, or when food intake and activity change.

## 9. Contraindications and Precautions

Metformin may be unsuitable or require special assessment in people with:

- Severe kidney impairment
- Metabolic acidosis, including diabetic ketoacidosis
- Severe dehydration or serious acute illness
- Significant liver disease or conditions associated with low oxygen
- Heavy or binge alcohol use
- A planned procedure involving iodinated contrast or major surgery

Tell the clinician about heart failure, recent heart attack or stroke, serious infection, breathing problems, alcohol use, pregnancy, breastfeeding and all other medicines.

Kidney function should be assessed before and during treatment as directed.

## 10. Drug and Procedure Interactions

Potentially important examples include:

- Iodinated contrast used for some imaging procedures: Temporary interruption and kidney-function reassessment may be required in certain patients.
- Some medicines that affect kidney function or metformin transport: May change metformin exposure.
- Alcohol: Can increase the risk of lactic acidosis, especially with heavy use.
- Other diabetes medicines, including insulin and sulfonylureas: May increase the risk of hypoglycaemia when used together.
- Certain diuretics and blood-pressure medicines may affect kidney function and require review.

Always tell healthcare professionals that you take metformin before surgery, imaging with contrast or a new prescription.

## 11. Missed Dose

Follow the prescription leaflet.

If a dose is missed, the usual advice is not to double the next dose. Take the next dose according to the regular schedule unless the prescriber or product leaflet says otherwise.

## 12. Monitoring

Monitoring may include:

- Kidney function (eGFR or other clinician-selected tests)
- Blood glucose and HbA1c
- Vitamin B12 in people with risk factors or suggestive symptoms
- Review of gastrointestinal tolerance and adherence
- Review of other medicines and acute-illness risks

The frequency of monitoring depends on health status and clinical guidance.

## 13. Storage and Handling

- Store in the original packaging according to the label.
- Protect from excess heat and moisture.
- Keep out of the reach and sight of children.
- Do not use expired tablets or liquid.
- Keep a current list of all medicines and supplements.

## 14. Patient Education Checklist

People taking metformin should understand:

1. It helps control type 2 diabetes but does not cure it.
2. It should be taken according to the prescribed schedule.
3. Kidney function monitoring is important.
4. Severe vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration or serious infection should prompt contact with the care team for sick-day instructions.
5. The care team must know about planned contrast imaging or surgery.
6. Symptoms suggestive of lactic acidosis require urgent medical assessment.
7. Long-term use may be associated with low vitamin B12.

## 15. When to Seek Medical Help

Seek urgent medical assessment for possible lactic acidosis symptoms, severe allergic reaction, chest pain, severe breathing difficulty or marked confusion/fainting.

Contact the diabetes care team for persistent gastrointestinal problems, repeated low glucose when taking combination therapy, dehydration, serious infection or questions about stopping and restarting treatment around procedures.

## 16. Key Facts for Document Search

- Metformin is a biguanide used mainly for type 2 diabetes.
- It reduces liver glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity.
- Common side effects are gastrointestinal.
- Kidney function should be assessed and monitored.
- Rare lactic acidosis is a serious safety warning.
- Long-term use may be associated with vitamin B12 deficiency.
- Contrast imaging and acute illness may require a clinician-directed plan.
